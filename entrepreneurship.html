Jeremy Haddock
Resume Self-driven MBA Writings & Projects

In 2024, having always had an entrepreneurial itch and seeking a more meaningful challenge, I set out to teach myself business by trying to build one. I expected to fail, and labeled what I thought at the time would be a 3-month stretch of learning by doing my self-driven MBA. My strategy was to get as many reps as possible in a short amount of time, and record the learnings for each in formal reflections. I set tight deadlines to go from idea through validating demand and securing revenue in days, not months. To my surprise, one of the ideas caught real traction, and I grew it to a $350k run rate business, with 18 clients and 6 team members over the course of two years. This is the story of how it happened and the twists, turns, and learnings along the way.

Everything below is dated and in order, including the parts that did not work, so that anyone who heard this from me in person can check that it holds up.

“I see these series of explorations almost like courses I’ve taken in my very-mini-MBA”

May 2024
May 2024

The declaration

No classroom, no degree — I would learn business by starting them, and I put that in writing before I started.

Summer 2024

My approach

The approach I took to business-building was to notice problems, size the potential demand, ideate a solution, prototype, present it to real customers and try to sell it. If a project withstood all of these stages, I considered this a real potential business that would be worth investing more time into. I followed this process for four ideas: an accessory to a best-selling baby chair, a sauna facilities product, a course teaching luxury housekeeping, and a lead generation agency.

Summer 2024

An accessory for a best-selling baby chair

My first idea was to build an accessory for an already proven product, a way to reduce risk to building a successful business in the same way that iPhone cases are huge businesses. The product I chose to accessorize was the Inglesina fast-clip baby chair, a best-selling product.

Summer 2024

Baby chair: finding friction

I combed Reddit threads discussing the baby chair, and found that most people loved the product because it allowed them to easily move their little ones to different locations without having to schlep a kids chair around. I came across a snap-on tray that allowed kids to eat in the chair, and this triggered my idea: a snap-on tray with built-in art features that allowed kids to be entertained and have their brains developed in the chair. I hit the streets with an AI image of my product, and gathered data to help size potential demand and whether there was interest in my product.

Summer 2024

Baby chair: testing demand and first pivot

Confused, impatient, embarrassed looks — I got a lot of those as I hawked down babysitters and moms with strollers on the West Side Highway of Manhattan. My goal was to show people my physical prototype, get feedback, and score a first sale. Although I got a lot of “great idea”s and “you’re onto something man”s, when I asked for someone to pay $20 for the prototype, everyone awkwardly declined. I learned that kids young enough to sit in the chair did not have the brain development to do art yet, plus would likely try to eat the art materials. I pivoted to a “busy board” concept instead, and got back out there, but this also failed to land. Ultimately, I invalidated the idea mostly due to insufficient demand.

Summer 2024

Baby chair: the challenge with consumer products

This was a first experience with the difficulty of building consumer products, where identifying problems consumers are willing to pay for is hard. I started looking for problems with economic costs I could more easily define, and where the clients were businesses, which I learned are more willing to spend money.

Summer 2024

Sauna Guard

Every day after working out, I used my gym’s sauna. One to two times per week, the sauna would be out of commission. The reason? People kept pouring water on the electric sauna heater to generate steam. I saw this as an opportunity to solve a maintenance problem for a business.

Summer 2024

Sauna Guard: sizing the market

To check it wasn’t a one-off, I called every gym in Manhattan, recording how many had electric saunas and how many had the problem. I learned that 70% of gyms had saunas and 50% of gyms with saunas experienced this same problem — with 113k gyms in the US I sized this TAM at $20M.

Summer 2024

Sauna Guard: prototype and selling

My next step was to build a prototype, in this case, a design sketch of a protective sauna heater enclosure meant to read like a professional contractor doc. With a prototype in hand, I started pounding the pavement, pitching an 800% ROI and three hour install to dozens of gym managers. Eventually, someone bit! I sold the promise to install a sauna enclosure for $1,000 at a major gym franchise with dozens of locations throughout the northeast. Scoring real revenue for the first time was a big milestone. Unfortunately, I didn’t predict the bumps in the road that came next.

Summer 2024

Sauna Guard: scaling issues and invalidation

I had sent the enclosure design to a carpenter and contracted the build. On delivery day, everything went wrong: the carpenter didn’t show up, the gym was frustrated by the delay, and the last minute replacement carpenter built the design completely incorrectly. I sat down on the sidewalk, head in hands, feeling like a fool. In the end, after a successful rebuild, we installed a value-additive product and got paid, but the issues with scaling this business were painfully clear. The thought of contracting hundreds of carpenters across the country and dealing with liability risk of a product involving wood next to hot metal, all on top of 20% gross margins and little meaningful differentiation didn’t exactly get me excited. I killed the business, took the learnings, and sought out an idea with simpler distribution and one where a brand could be built.

Summer 2024

Palm Beach Luxury Housekeeping Course

I came across a viral article that reported that Palm Beach luxury housekeepers were earning $150k a year on a supply shortage. This story was reported by Business Insider, Fortune, NY Post, CNBC, and many others. I interpreted this as a strong demand signal and shortcut to validating demand. Many of the articles quoted the leader of the most prestigious housekeeper agency in Florida, named April Berube, who mentioned that she thought a course was the best solution to increase the supply of skilled labor. I had confirmed demand and had what was likely a good solution, so I got to work building the landing page for the Palm Beach Luxury Housekeeping Course.

Summer 2024

Palm Beach course: marketing strategy and first sales

My strategy to sell the course was twofold: a social media marketing campaign via Facebook groups, and winning referral partnerships with staffing agencies who had hundreds of job-seeking housekeepers on their email lists. The social media campaign didn’t yield great results, but cold outreach scored me a partnership with a smaller agency, who included my course announcement in one of their email blasts. This got us our first 15 students for two courses, or about $4k in revenue.

Summer 2024

Palm Beach course: course design and delighting customers

I put out a job ad and recruited a charismatic, experienced housekeeper named Fernanda to be my course instructor and co-designer of the curriculum. I got discouraged early on, ostensibly realizing that the only course sites we could afford were soul-sucking, fluorescent hotel conference rooms for $250/h. A bunch of people sitting around a table watching a PowerPoint slide… ugh. A major lightbulb moment was realizing that we could rent an entire villa for $250/night, allowing us to instruct the course with real props like a bed and dining table, as well as have an environment where students could let their guard down and build real relationships. Having unexpected surprises like professional headshots, handwritten notes, and a thoughtful gift bag were intentional decisions to delight customers and get great reviews. Making the course feel rigorous with a printed certificate of graduation that had to be earned, and asking the students to have professional outfits that we would judge, were also intentional choices to make the course a rewarding experience. We delighted our customers and got 100% 5-star reviews all while turning a profit.

Summer 2024

Palm Beach course: shutting it down

One of our students doubled her income within a month of the course and credited us with being instrumental in that. Coincidentally, the agency that staffed her was April Berube’s, the owner quoted in the viral articles. April caught wind of our course and called me, offering to incubate the course under her brand and with her capital. The decision to not continue building the course was mostly driven by opportunity cost — at the time I had already begun validating another idea which was showing promise and had the potential to be 10–20x as large. Additionally, I estimated the course could generate $200k/year in Florida, but was less confident about future growth or scaling it beyond Florida. I also had concerns that using agency referrals wasn’t a scalable customer acquisition strategy, which introduced more risk to an opportunity with a low ceiling. For all of those reasons I decided not to continue with the course, but still keep in touch with Fernanda and the students and am very proud of how we impacted their careers.

August 2024 Promise Kept catches traction — all other projects dropped

Promise Kept Company

I met another entrepreneur interested in the solar market. The pull was a federal programme, the REAP grant, which heavily incentivised commercial solar in rural areas. We saw room for a lead generation agency in that gap.

September 2024

First lead gen client

After some testing we sold our first batch — five leads at $200 each, paid on delivery. Each lead took roughly two and a half hours of calling.

December 2024

Brand and price

I wanted to position the service as high-trust and premium in a space full of scammy operators, which is why we incorporated as Promise Kept Solar, LLC. My big early mistake was underpricing and being too flexible on terms: payment on delivery instead of upfront, below-market rates, a lenient refund policy, lax invoicing. Our leads closed at 3–4% on a $100k average deal, so $200 was far below the expected value. We raised to $300, then $500 plus revenue share, then $1,000 plus revenue share — but too slowly.

January 2025

First experience hiring

We began hiring on the hypothesis that we could find lower-cost labour with English accents in South Africa. After moving on from that and testing other overseas options, we settled on US-based cold callers and started to see results. The early lessons were expensive: performance wasn't tracked closely, team members were siloed, incentives were weak, and my people management was subpar.

July 2025

Pivot to commercial IT

After running the solar lead business profitably for months and driving 100x-plus returns for some clients, the administration passed a bill that threatened the solar industry. We pivoted into commercial IT, a vertical with real synergies where our competitors already operated. That meant rebranding and bootstrapping credibility from zero: we recorded video testimonials from every solar client and made all industry-identifying details generic, and offered free IT leads to early clients in exchange for reviews and testimonials. By August we had our first managed-services clients, on a new model — $4–6k upfront on a seven-to-twelve-month minimum.

Fall 2025

Growing pains in IT

Delivering leads in IT was harder than solar, and harder to hire for. We ran through several recruiting, interviewing, and compensation strategies looking for consistent performers, and grew the team to a maximum of six.

Fall 2025

Brand shift

We needed a more credible brand. We rented an office, hired actors, and shot a video that made it look like we had an in-person presence. It massively raised our close rate.

Winter 2026

What B2B sales actually requires

Long contracts, higher prices, and long IT sales cycles meant months could pass with clients growing uneasy about results — which created a need for account management, to hold the relationship together and improve renewal odds. In parallel we worked on our own conversion: hired third parties to teach us B2B strategy, entered competitors' funnels to study their process, and poached competitors' employees to learn it directly. Conversion went from 7% to 12%.

Spring 2026

Fine-tuning the offer structure

We introduced contractual structures that made working with us look lower-risk while holding or increasing our upside.

Summer 2026

Peak closing performance

Eventually we cracked hiring for the internal funnel, sharpened the offer, and polished the closing tactics. In our final two months we were closing $25k of contract value a month.

Summer 2026

The decision to wind down

The internal funnel worked well; the external one never fully did. Two failed full-time US hires back to back cost the company cash and cost my morale more, which raised the temperature on client frustration and made the business draining to run. I also noticed a fraction of clients were seeing most of the results despite roughly equal lead quality. I spoke to clients about their closing processes and concluded we were delivering top-of-funnel sales but being judged on closing processes that were flawed and outside our control. Extrapolating that forward — a steady share of clients seeing negative returns, those relationships to manage, contracts to renew, a high-churn team, aggressive sales tactics — was not a future I was excited about. Paired with two years of significant personal sacrifice, I decided not to continue.

2026

I started this walking around the Meatpacking District being dodged by concerned mothers, waving a baby tray at them. I ended it personally closing $300k in contracts and employing full-time staff.

The 2022 note called each attempt a course. Read back now that framing was about right, and slightly generous to itself: the tuition was real, and so is what I know about selling to strangers, hiring, pricing, and telling when a thing is finished.
